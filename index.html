Five-stage RISC-V processor from Verilog to DRC- and LVS-clean GDSII in SkyWater 130 nm

A five-stage pipelined RV32I core taken from RTL all the way to a finished chip layout using only open-source tools: verified against an independent reference model, functional coverage and formal proofs, then synthesized, placed, routed and signed off for timing at three process corners, DRC and LVS.

  • Pipeline: five in-order stages across four synthesizable Verilog modules, with forwarding into execute from both the EX/MEM and MEM/WB registers, a write-through register file that removes the third forwarding path, a one-cycle load-use stall, and predict-not-taken branches with a two-cycle flush. Measured 0.869 instructions per cycle over 39,244 retired instructions.
  • Simulation verification: a separate Python RISC-V simulator written from the specification without looking at the RTL, itself checked against all 40 official rv32ui compliance programs. A cocotb testbench compares all sixteen retirement fields of every instruction against it, per instruction rather than per cycle, so stalls and flushes cannot hide an error. 144 programs, 39,244 instructions, zero mismatches, and 103 of 103 functional coverage bins.
  • Formal proofs, and the bug simulation could not find: five SymbiYosys property suites, two proved unbounded by k-induction. The core-level proof found that a branch target can land 2 past a word boundary and send fetch to an address that holds no instruction. No assembler or test generator emits such a target, so no amount of random stimulus would have reached it. The solver found it because instruction memory was a free variable. The RTL now traps on it, as the specification requires.
  • Synthesis and layout: Yosys and ABC onto the sky130_fd_sc_hd library gave 5,811 cells and 1,375 flip-flops, and the synthesized netlist matches the reference model across 45 programs. A Python-driven OpenROAD flow runs floorplan through routing and extraction, ending in a 424.58 × 427.04 µm die with 8,059 nets and 315,103 µm of wire, and zero router DRC violations after seven routing passes.
  • Timing at three corners: signing off at the typical corner alone looked finished and was not. The same layout failed setup by 15.38 ns at the slow corner and hold by 47 ps at the fast corner, and a hold failure cannot be fixed by slowing the clock. Rebuilding the flow to optimize against all three corners closed both, at +0.8409 ns setup at the slow corner and +0.0945 ns hold at the fast corner, using 3.03 mW. That gives two honest frequencies: 69.0 MHz on typical silicon and 32.3 MHz guaranteed across process, voltage and temperature. The limiting structure is a 29-gate ripple-carry chain in the adder.
  • Signoff: the independent KLayout design rule deck found 64 minimum-area violations on met3 that the router’s own check reported as zero. A repair step grows each undersized pad on the manufacturing grid without breaking spacing, giving zero violations, and layout versus schematic matches across 10,422 instances.

Not everything is clean, and the report says so: 129 slew and 8 capacitance violations remain, 12 antenna nets are unrepaired, parasitic extraction is single-corner, and there is no metal fill. It is a core macro, not a whole chip.

4x4 INT8 systolic-array matrix multiplier from SystemVerilog to a signed-off layout in SkyWater 130 nm

The block AI accelerators are built from: a grid of INT8 multiply-accumulate units with 32-bit accumulators, verified against an independent reference model and taken through synthesis, place-and-route, extraction, three-corner timing closure, DRC and LVS. The 4x4 closes setup and hold at the slow, typical and fast corners at 100 MHz, and the same parameterized RTL was also implemented as 2x2 and 8x8 to see how it scales.

  • Architecture: output-stationary dataflow, so accumulators stay in place and only 8-bit operands move between neighbours; valid/ready streaming ports; a “first” flag that rides with the operands reloads each accumulator at the start of a tile, so there is no clear cycle between tiles.
  • A latency I got wrong by a factor of two: I mutation-tested my own RTL. Shortening the post-input drain by one cycle should have failed and did not. The derivation, not the testbench, was wrong: results leave one row per cycle and row 0 finishes long before the far corner, so N cycles is enough, not 2N. One cycle less than N fails at every size, which proves the bound tight.
  • Verification: a cocotb testbench with random stalls on both ports, and random garbage on the operand buses whenever the input is idle, checked 115,164 results over 4,113 tiles at three array sizes with zero mismatches. The same tests pass on the synthesized and routed netlists.
  • A slow-corner miss that was not about the clock: the first layout missed by the same few tens of picoseconds at two different periods. The repair tool was stopping at zero against its own parasitic estimate and the real extraction decided the result. Margins, slow-corner synthesis and removing a pointless 64-bit operand mux fixed it; a sweep of complete P&R runs puts the limit between 9.5 and 10 ns.
  • Power and placement: with switching activity from a gate-level run on random INT8 data, the 4x4 draws 17.1 mW at the typical corner, 16.1 pJ per MAC, and registers plus the clock tree cost more than the arithmetic. The placer laid each accumulator out as a strand along its carry chain and grouped PEs by output column, pulled by the 128-bit result port rather than the systolic links.
  • Signoff: the KLayout rule deck found 63 met3 minimum-area violations the router missed; all patched, zero remain. LVS matches at all three sizes after five fixes, four of them in the checking flow, and two deliberate canary faults (a swapped flip-flop connection, a deleted cell) both make it fail.

The report lists what is still open: accumulators are not double-buffered, so the 4x4 uses 67% of its MAC slots at K = 16; extraction is single-corner; the 8x8 closes at 91 MHz but still carries slew and capacitance violations; there is no metal fill. It is a macro, not a whole chip.

Custom 32-bit RISC-V processor and assembly software system

I built a 32-bit RISC-V processor from the datapath up and wrote assembly software to run on it, then deployed it to an FPGA where the finished system reads ten values from hardware switches, computes a quotient and remainder for each, sorts both result arrays, and drives the answers out to a seven-segment display and LEDs.

  • Datapath: a 32-bit ALU covering add, subtract, AND, OR, XOR, shifts and signed and unsigned comparison; a 32 by 32-bit register file with dual asynchronous read ports and correct hardwired-zero handling; program counter logic for sequential execution, branches and jumps; immediate generation with sign extension for every instruction format; and branch condition and address generation.
  • Control: a multi-cycle state machine driving fetch, decode, execute, memory access and writeback, with a decoder generating control signals from the opcode and function fields across all six RISC-V instruction formats.
  • Software: the processor has no hardware multiply or divide, so I wrote division as repeated subtraction and multiplication as repeated addition. On top of that, array processing with pointer arithmetic, a bubble sort with nested loops and in-place swapping, and subroutines with manual register saving.
  • Integration: memory-mapped I/O to switches, LEDs and seven-segment displays, with programs loaded into instruction memory via $readmemh.

I verified it first in waveform simulation, checking timing and signal propagation across every datapath component, then on the board, where the failures were different in character: timing mismatches, input synchronization, control signals arriving a cycle off. Validated end to end against a fixed dataset with known expected output.

Digital logic systems and FSM-based access control

I built a parking access controller that validates a passcode through state machine logic and tracks live occupancy with a counter, so the gate opens only when the code is right and there is capacity left, integrating the FSM, counter, gate logic and display modules into one top-level design.

This is the project where digital design stopped being truth tables for me and became architecture. The access controller has two independent pieces of state that have to agree before anything happens: where you are in entering the passcode, which the state machine tracks, and how many cars are already inside, which a separate counter module tracks. The gate logic sits downstream and only asserts when the FSM has reached its accept state and the counter says there is room. Splitting those into separate modules rather than one tangled block is the whole point, because occupancy and authentication are unrelated concerns that happen to gate the same output.

  • Why minimization is not just an exercise. I derived minimized sum-of-products and product-of-sums expressions from truth tables and K-maps, then implemented the same function two ways, gate-level and NOR-only, to show they behave identically. NOR is functionally complete, meaning you can build any logic function from nothing but NOR gates, which matters because a real process often gives you one cheap universal gate rather than a full library. Fewer terms means fewer gates means less area and less propagation delay.
  • Overflow detection, and why sign bits are enough. The 4-bit signed calculator uses two’s complement with ripple carry adders and multiplexers for operand selection. The interesting part is the validity checker. In two’s complement, if you add two positive numbers and get a negative result, or two negatives and get a positive, you have overflowed, and you can detect that purely from the sign bits of the operands and the result. No extra arithmetic. When it fires, I suppress the display rather than showing a confidently wrong answer, which felt like the right call for anything a person reads off a screen.
  • Mealy versus Moore, from the waveforms rather than the textbook. I built both, with overlapping and non-overlapping sequence detection. The difference shows up clearly in simulation: a Mealy machine’s output depends on the current state and the current input, so it reacts in the same cycle and usually needs fewer states. A Moore machine’s output depends only on state, so it lags by a cycle but is stable for the whole cycle and does not glitch when the input moves. Overlapping detection matters when the tail of one match can serve as the head of the next, which changes where your transitions point.
  • Making 100 MHz visible. The board clock is 100 MHz, which is invisible on an LED. I built a cascaded chain of D flip-flops where each stage divides the frequency by two, scaling it down into the sub-Hz range so behavior becomes something you can watch and count. I verified the division ratio and the synchronization between stages in waveform analysis rather than trusting the arithmetic, since a clock divider that is off by a stage looks fine until it does not.
  • Building arithmetic from primitives. Half adder, then full adder, then a 4-bit ripple carry adder, modeling how carry propagates stage to stage. Ripple carry is the slow way to add, because each stage waits on the carry from the one below it, and seeing that delay accumulate in simulation is the reason carry-lookahead exists.
  • Getting it onto real pins. Everything above only matters if it reaches hardware, which means constraints files mapping logical signal names to physical FPGA pins for switches, buttons, LEDs and the seven-segment displays, then synthesis, implementation and bitstream generation.

The access controller is the project I would point at if you wanted to see whether I can decompose a system rather than just write modules.

8-bit SAR ADC, transistor-level design through layout and post-layout verification in SkyWater 130 nm

A fully differential 8-bit, 10 MS/s successive-approximation ADC designed at the transistor level, simulated across eleven PVT corners and Monte Carlo, laid out with a layout generator I wrote, and verified DRC- and LVS-clean. The extracted layout reaches 7.89 effective bits near Nyquist at 235 µW, a Walden figure of merit of 99 fJ per conversion step.

  • Choosing 8 bits: a 2000-trial Monte Carlo of the exact switching algorithm using the PDK’s own capacitor matching showed 3x3 µm unit capacitors keep the DAC alone at 0.20 LSB worst-case DNL, so 8 bits is not mismatch-limited while comparator offset, noise and layout parasitics still show up at the LSB level.
  • Circuits: monotonic switch-up capacitor switching with top-plate sampling, which halves the array; a StrongARM comparator with an NMOS input pair because the common mode rises toward the supply; bootstrapped sampling switches; and SAR logic from 33 standard cells. Simulation showed the MSB drivers settling too slowly, so I resized them until every bit settles to 0.1 LSB within 1.78 ns.
  • Corners and mismatch: eleven PVT corners give 7.43 to 7.81 effective bits, comparator offset Monte Carlo gives σ = 3.17 mV, and capacitor-mismatch Monte Carlo keeps 100% of 2000 trials below 0.5 LSB DNL.
  • A layout that shifted every code by 7 LSB: the first comparator layout had 2.4 fF of asymmetric output capacitance from generic routing, which became a common-mode-dependent offset of up to 60 mV. A mirror-symmetric layout fixed most of it; two controlled re-simulations then traced the rest to an extraction artifact that double-counted diffusion across mirrored sub-cells, and flat extraction brought the offset under 1.75 mV.
  • Signoff: Magic DRC taken from thousands of error tiles to zero, the independent KLayout deck at zero with a canary cell proving it catches real errors, and netgen LVS matching uniquely. LVS caught a real short between the bootstrap capacitor and an MSB control line that DRC could not see.

One open item, stated in the report: the schematic-level DNL/INL of 0.62/0.58 LSB misses the 0.5 LSB target at major carries while the extracted layout reaches 0.16/0.11. The DAC, comparator hysteresis, timestep and solver tolerance are ruled out; the cause is still being traced.

Nine-stage electronic chain reaction machine

I got nine analog, digital and electromechanical stages firing in sequence for a live demonstration, after isolating six separate faults during integration that each broke the chain at a different link.

The sequence: a capacitive touch piano reads a five-note code and closes a relay. The relay isolates 5 V logic from a 9 V strobe circuit that steps up to roughly 580 V and fires a xenon tube through an SCR. A Schmitt-trigger light detector catches the flash and fires a solenoid, which launches a steel marble into a 555 timer metal detector coil. The marble’s inductance shifts the oscillator from 8.76 kHz down to 8.31 kHz. The Arduino sees that 5.1% drop and cuts a MOSFET-driven electromagnet, dropping a ball onto a tilt switch, which fills the LCD to confirm the whole thing fired.

  • Strobe would not fire. The relay was switching fine, so the problem was past it: a broken lead on one of the oscillator transistors driving the step-up transformer, which killed the high voltage charging stage entirely.
  • Light detector triggering on room lights. The photoresistor divider had the reference sitting too low. Raising the threshold meant only the strobe flash crossed it, and the Schmitt trigger’s hysteresis kept it from chattering at the boundary.
  • Electromagnet releasing the instant somebody touched a piano key. The Arduino was reacting to small frequency noise in the 555 before the marble was anywhere near the coil. I added a streak counter requiring several consecutive low readings, a release delay, and a retuned threshold.
  • MOSFET not switching at all. The Arduino ran on USB 5 V, the electromagnet on a separate 9 V supply, and the two grounds were never tied together, so there was no defined gate-source voltage. Connecting them fixed it immediately.
  • Metal detector threshold. Rather than computing percent change live, I set a fixed trip delta and tuned it until false triggers stopped without losing real detections.

Built with Arya Geejo and Amogh Somisetty. The report also documents an end-of-life plan separating what gets reused from what needs e-waste handling, which was part of the brief.

12 V to 3.3 V synchronous buck converter design study (TI TPS62130A)

A 12 V to 3.3 V, 0 to 2 A point-of-load converter design built around the TI TPS62130A, carried from datasheet verification and hand calculation through circuit simulation, loss modeling, schematic and layout review, board implementation, and an automated bench-test framework.

  • Design calculations: checked against the TI datasheet, with a feedback divider of 750 kΩ and 240 kΩ for 3.300 V. Volt-second balance including switch and inductor drops gives a duty cycle of 0.288 at 2 A rather than the ideal 0.275, and because the part uses a fixed on-time, that raises the switching frequency to about 2.618 MHz. Inductor ripple is 435 mA peak to peak with a 2.29 A peak against a 3.8 A rating. The 22 µF output capacitor keeps only about half its capacitance at 3.3 V, which doubles the capacitive ripple term. Estimated efficiency is 90.0% at 12 V and 2 A, with a 736 mW loss budget.
  • Simulation: TI does not publish a model of its DCS-Control loop, so the ngspice model is a generic voltage-mode buck with a Type III compensator (149 kHz crossover, 57° phase margin at 12 V) and real parasitics. Seven steady-state points, a 0.5 to 1.5 A load step and startup all ran. Three simulation artifacts were found and fixed before any result was accepted, including ideal switches producing picosecond edges and impossible 18 V spikes on the 12 V rail. After the fixes, simulated capacitor-only ripple matched the hand calculation, 2.03 mV against 1.98 mV.
  • Loss model: conduction in both FETs and the inductor, switching overlap, dead-time, gate-drive and quiescent loss, plus a separate power-save model, with a breakdown by mechanism at 0.2, 1 and 2 A. The simulation is forced continuous-conduction with no gate-drive loss, so its 93.6 to 97.6% efficiency is optimistic, and the report says so.
  • Schematic and layout review: the schematic was drawn in code, with a parts list, and a to-scale placement follows the VQFN-16 pinout with a 5.8 mm² input hot loop, a nine-via thermal array and a Kelvin-sensed output. The layout checklist is written as code. The first placement failed the input-capacitor distance check because the pinout was mirrored, and the second passed. This is a planning exercise: nothing was routed, design-rule checked or sent to a fab.
  • Test framework: Python and PyVISA with abstract instrument interfaces, SCPI drivers for a Keysight-style bench, and mock instruments so the whole suite runs without hardware. Five scripts cover efficiency, regulation with a thermal soak, scope captures, load transient and startup. The method includes Kelvin-sensed voltages so lead drops stay out of the efficiency figure, a series meter for input current because a supply readback is useless at light load, and a shorted-probe noise-floor capture before trusting any millivolt ripple reading.
  • Prediction versus model: 17 quantities ranked by the gap between prediction and the modeled result, each with a physical explanation. Input ripple, for example, comes out near 90 mV against 37.7 mV from the formula, because capacitor ESL turns each roughly 4 ns current edge into a voltage spike the formula leaves out. The load-step response is the least certain result, since the generic loop is slower than DCS-Control.

AC circuits, power factor correction and frequency response

I brought an inductive load from a 0.694 lagging power factor up to 0.999 by adding capacitive correction, pulling the phase angle from about 46 degrees down to near zero and confirming it visually on the scope.

Power factor is one of those things that sounds like bookkeeping until you see what it costs. An inductive load pulls current out of phase with voltage, so some of the current flowing through your conductors is doing no useful work. It still heats the wire, still needs the conductor sized for it, and utilities still bill industrial customers for it. Adding capacitance supplies that reactive component locally instead of dragging it back through the supply. Going from 0.694 to 0.999 means almost all the current is now doing real work.

  • The measurement I liked most. Put voltage on one scope channel and current on the other, switch to XY mode, and you get a Lissajous figure. Any phase difference draws an ellipse. As you correct the power factor the ellipse gets narrower, and at unity it collapses into a straight line. You can watch correction happen without reading a single number, which is a much better intuition than a calculated angle.
  • Resonance, and why the impedance goes up. In a parallel RLC circuit, inductive and capacitive reactance cancel at resonance, and what is left is the resistance alone, so impedance peaks instead of dropping. I measured that peak near 147 ohms and found resonance at about 1000 Hz against 1023 Hz theoretical. From the width of that peak you get bandwidth, and from the ratio of resonant frequency to bandwidth you get Q, which is really a statement about how much energy the tank stores versus how much it burns per cycle. Low Q means a lossy, broad, heavily damped resonance.
  • A measurement gap that located itself. My RC filter cutoff came in around 130 Hz against 159 Hz theoretical, which is a big miss. But LTSpice agreed with theory to within 2 to 3%. That is the useful part: if simulation matches the math and the bench does not, the error is not in my analysis, it is in the physical parts. Component tolerance and probe loading, not algebra. Being able to say which of the three is wrong is more valuable than any single number.
  • Why an unbalanced three-phase system costs you. With balanced loads the three phase currents sum to zero at the neutral, so there is no neutral current and nothing wasted there. Unbalance the loads and that cancellation stops working: current flows in the neutral, phase currents go unequal, and losses climb. I watched efficiency fall from about 98.5% to 93.3% doing exactly that.
  • Maximum power transfer, measured rather than assumed. I swept load resistance and measured dissipation to find the actual peak instead of trusting the textbook match condition, then accounted for the offset using non-ideal source impedance and component parasitics.
  • Two-port characterization. I extracted Z, Y and H parameter matrices from open-circuit and short-circuit measurements. The idea is to describe a network entirely by how it behaves at its terminals, so you can drop it into a larger system without caring what is inside it. Which set you use depends on which terminal conditions are convenient to actually create on the bench.
  • I also verified Kirchhoff’s voltage law in the phasor domain, comparing measured voltages against vector calculations rather than scalar sums, and confirmed conservation of real, reactive and apparent power around the circuit.

This is measurement-heavy work, so the output lives in lab data, plots and error analysis rather than in anything filmable. Happy to walk through any of the datasets.

PC to board RS-485 control path on a new MCU architecture

I got a dead control link working end to end by ruling out every software cause one at a time, which narrowed a silent receive interrupt down to hardware and surfaced a documented board rework that had never been applied to that particular unit.

  • This board used the FRAM-based MSP430FR5964 instead of the part I had been working on, so I had to adapt to different clock configuration, different peripheral register naming, and a different serial module setup.

Relay multiplexer controller for burn-in test equipment

I replaced a channel switching procedure technicians were doing by hand with a controller they now use as standard equipment on the burn-in bench, delivered on schedule as firmware plus a Python interface, and written up in a commendation from the supervising staff engineer.

Some context if burn-in is not your area. Burn-in means running parts hard, at elevated temperature and voltage, to force early-life failures out before product ships. You have many devices under test and one set of measurement instruments, so you need to switch which device the instruments are looking at. That switching was being done manually. My job was to make it a command instead of a procedure, which meant a relay bank the host could drive over RS-485.

  • Reverse-engineering the relay encoding. There are three relay control lines but they do not behave as three independent bits. Bench testing showed two of them are driven together as a single low-order bit while the third acts as an independent high-order bit. That gives four usable positions, and I built the position table around how the hardware actually behaves rather than how three lines ought to behave.
  • Polarity, and defending a bench result. I determined the relays were active-low empirically, using an LED inversion test on the actual board. An automated code review then told me to reverse it, with a plausible-sounding rationale. I pushed back twice and kept the measured behavior. Accepting that change would have inverted every relay in the system, which on burn-in equipment means connecting the wrong device to live instruments. Measured beats inferred.
  • The interlock, and why it needed proof. The engineering lead specified that no more than two relay banks may be energized at once, because relay coils draw real current and the supply has a ceiling. Rather than assume my position table respected that, I verified it mathematically across all four commands and checked it programmatically before flashing. When that change landed it also invalidated a bench result I had already recorded for one position, so I re-ran that validation instead of assuming the old measurement still held.
  • The self-echo bug. Replies started coming back consistently one command behind. One command behind is not noise, it is protocol state. RS-485 is half duplex, so everything shares a single differential pair and a board hears its own transmissions on its own receiver. The firmware was treating its outgoing reply as a fresh incoming command. I fixed it on both ends: the firmware waits for the transmit-complete flag and discards the echoed byte, and the host drains its receive buffer.
  • Safe defaults and a local override. Nothing energizes at power-up until the host explicitly commands a position. I also added an onboard push button so a technician can advance the mux at the bench without a computer, with a deliberately long software debounce of about a second, sampled off the periodic timer. A second is far too slow for most debounce, but this is a mechanical control a person presses, so slow and certain beats fast and twitchy.
  • I also found that the timer interrupt was not clearing low-power mode on return, which would have left button presses sitting unprocessed until something else woke the chip, and that the acknowledgment LED flash lasted a single tick, too short for anyone to see.

One more thing I would do the same way again: I held the release rather than flashing an assumed button pin mapping. The assumption turned out to be wrong, so waiting for confirmation prevented a defect with physical consequences. All the hardware-dependent constants, pin mappings, active-level values and the position table, live in one marked config block so any of it is a one-line change. Deployed to a second workstation and demoed live to the technicians who use it.

3 kHz waveform generation firmware driving a parallel DAC

I caught a wiring assumption that would have quietly produced wrong output, by tracing all eight DAC data lines through the schematic netlist before flashing anything and finding the byte split non-sequentially across two GPIO ports.

Some context on what this board does. Most of my other work on this platform was the microcontroller reading things. Here it was the only signal source: the MCU drives an 8-bit parallel multiplying DAC, and whatever byte it writes becomes the analog output. Downstream of the DAC is a two-stage OPA991 network doing current-to-voltage conversion and then AC coupling, which shifts the DAC’s unipolar output into a bipolar swing of roughly plus or minus 2.5 V.

  • Why the pin mapping mattered so much. The obvious move is to build a byte and write it to one port. On this board the eight data lines are scattered: three bits on one port, three on another, two back on the first, and not in order. A single-port write would still hand the DAC a perfectly valid 8-bit code, just not the code I meant. So the output would have been a stable, repeating, completely wrong waveform. That is the worst kind of bug, because it looks analog. Somebody would have spent a day probing the op-amp stage chasing a fault that was purely digital. I escalated it as a blocking question and got the mapping confirmed before I flashed anything.
  • Why lookup tables instead of computing the sine. At 48 kHz sample rate the interrupt fires roughly every 20 microseconds. An MSP430 at 16 MHz with no floating point unit cannot evaluate a sine function in that window. So I generate the table once at startup, and critically I generate it already bit-shuffled into the two port layouts the board actually needs. That moves all the bit manipulation out of the hot path, leaving the interrupt with two port writes and an index increment.
  • A trade-off I documented rather than hid. The DAC’s chip select and write strobe are tied to ground, which I confirmed in the netlist. That means it is transparent: the output tracks the input continuously instead of latching on a strobe. So writing two ports in sequence means the DAC briefly sees a half-updated code, for well under a microsecond. I wrote that up and assessed it as negligible against the analog filtering downstream, but it is the kind of thing worth stating explicitly rather than letting somebody discover later.
  • Why 96 kHz. The reviewing engineer asked for less stair-stepping. More samples per cycle means smaller voltage steps between them, so I moved from 16 points at 48 kHz to a 32-point table at 96 kHz. Same 3 kHz output, finer approximation, and the clock was configured at 16 MHz specifically to leave headroom for the faster interrupt rate.
  • The heartbeat trick. Before trusting anything on the analog side I added an independent bicolor LED blinking at about 2 Hz off the same timer chain. If that blinks, the clock is configured, the timer is counting, and interrupts are firing. It costs nothing and it separates “my firmware is not running” from “my firmware runs and the analog path is wrong,” which are very different problems to debug.

I also fixed a recurring workflow fault where the debugger kept launching the wrong project, and established a reliable project-specific flash and debug procedure so revisions stopped being a gamble.

Verification status, stated plainly: the digital timing path was confirmed on hardware through the heartbeat, proving the clock, timer and interrupt chain worked, and the manager observed the output and asked for the smoothing revision, which I delivered. The final waveform frequency, amplitude and centering were not oscilloscope-measured before handoff. I wrote that distinction into the handoff documentation rather than letting it read as fully validated, because “delivered” and “measured” are not the same claim.

Environmental monitoring system

I built a monitor that tracks temperature, humidity, light level and motion and surfaces all four on a single display, using a hardware interrupt for motion so the alert fires the instant the sensor trips rather than waiting for the main loop to come back around.

  • The display alternates every five seconds between temperature and humidity, then light level and motion status, which fits four readings onto a two-line screen without cramming.
  • An RGB LED bands the light level by color, red when it is dim, blue through the middle, green when it is bright, so you get the reading without looking at a number.
  • Raw analog light values jitter enough that the color kept flickering at the band edges, so I put a weighted-average smoothing filter on the photoresistor readings.
  • Motion runs on attachInterrupt on a rising edge instead of polling. The interrupt sets a flag and a timestamp, and the main loop clears the alert two seconds later, so the alert never blocks anything else.

I scoped it as a product rather than a demo, so the writeup includes market research against Nest, Ecobee, Airthings, Govee and Philips Hue, a defined user and operating environment, quantified performance requirements, and an itemized $63 bill of materials.

Electrical layout review and load analysis across client projects

I cut design revision cycles by 15% across more than 10 client projects, by reviewing and updating electrical layouts in AutoCAD so the drawings actually matched as-built conditions instead of what was originally specified.

The core problem here is the gap between designed and as-built. A drawing gets approved, then the field runs into a conduit route that does not work, or a panel location that conflicts with something structural, and the installation deviates. If nobody feeds that back into the drawing, the next revision is authored against a building that no longer exists, and you get a second revision cycle caused entirely by stale information. Closing that loop is where the 15% came from.

  • Ran electrical load analysis and power distribution calculations in Excel. Those numbers feed straight into cost estimation and into sizing decisions, so getting them settled early is what let design sign-off move faster on client deliverables.
  • Delivered projects with zero specification mismatches by coordinating between contractors, engineers and clients. Most mismatches I saw were not technical disagreements, they were three parties each holding a slightly different version of the truth. Getting design intent and field requirements aligned before installation is much cheaper than discovering the gap afterward.
  • Worked across full project lifecycles rather than one slice of them, which is where I learned that a drawing being correct and a drawing being current are two different problems and only one of them shows up in review.

Consulting work under client projects, so there is nothing here I can publish. Happy to talk through the load analysis methodology in an interview.
